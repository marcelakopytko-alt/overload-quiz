    Part 2 — Three questions worth sitting with

    From the talk's closing questions. Answer honestly and watch the counter.

    What is my professional edge — and is AI strengthening it, or slowly weakening it?

    Am I giving AI the low-value tasks and keeping the high-value ones — or the other way round?

    If I felt overloaded, would I say so out loud — or quietly ask AI instead?

    Open communication is the principle that matters here: feeling safe to talk about AI use and overload. If that is missing, no amount of personal habit change fixes it — it needs to come from the top.

    Running this with a team

    Ten minutes, no slides. The room tells you more than the individual results do.

    The 10-minute team version
    • Silent first (3 min). Everyone answers on their own device. No discussion yet — discussion contaminates the result.
    • One sentence each. Everyone names only their primary type. No justifying, no ranking, no "but actually I have all five".
    • Tally the room. Five types on a board, one mark against each name.
    • Read the tally, not the individual. If most of the room lands on attention, cognitive or recovery, that is a norm problem, not a training problem — and the fixes are team-level: focus time, meeting hygiene, availability windows.
    • Each person commits to one or two adjustments (Part 3), said out loud. Then re-run the quiz in a month and compare the tally.
    The misdiagnosis to watch for

    Attention overload masquerading as an information problem. When people say "there is too much to read", the cause is usually the tool-switching that stopped them reading it. Reduce the number of open streams before you build a better reading list.

    Skill drift masquerading as efficiency. If you are faster and less capable than last year, the second half of that sentence is the part that matters.

    The three questions the talk left open
    • How do we make sure people still ask questions if they can ask AI instead? It has to come from the top: leaders asking, visibly not knowing, and rewarding the question.
    • How do we protect against cognitive overload? Work in blocks of focus time and minimise the distractions around them. It is a calendar decision before it is a willpower decision.
    • What about colleagues whose feedback sounds like it came from AI? A verbal conversation, not a written accusation. Be open about it, and celebrate the human-ness when you find it.
